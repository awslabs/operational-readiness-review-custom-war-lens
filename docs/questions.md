# The ORR lens family, question by question

Generated by `python3 -m tools.orrlens docs` from `lens-src/`. Do not edit by hand.
The JSON files in `dist/` are generated from the same sources.

Risk rules are evaluated top to bottom and the first true condition sets the risk. Tiers: a scope exit scores No risk on its own; core statements are needed to avoid High; one statement of each alternative group is needed to avoid High; secondary statements are needed only for No risk. "None of these" (`none_no`) appears only in the guard `&& !none_no` on the No risk and Medium rules (sandbox gate G1), so selecting it, alone or with other choices, scores the question's maximum risk.

<details>
<summary><code>rp_dataplane_recovery</code>: Failover uses data-plane actions on pre-provisioned resources, and no recovery step creates resources in the impaired location</summary>

Good looks like: Every failover step moves traffic to, or changes state on, resources that already exist and are sized for the load (for example Route 53 health-check failover records or an ARC zonal shift), with no launches, resource changes, or IAM or DNS edits through a control plane. Under an approved backup-and-restore or pilot-light strategy, steps that create or scale resources run only in the recovery Region or account, need neither the impaired Region nor a global service control plane, fit inside RTO, and were proven in the last DR test. Capacity to survive losing an Availability Zone is scored in event_resilience_recoveries (az_static_capacity). Platform notes: Serverless: size concurrency and quotas in the recovery location ahead of time, and never deploy functions or stacks into the impaired Region during recovery. Partition notes: ARC routing control is not available in AWS GovCloud (US) as of 2026-10-05; Route 53 failover routing and ARC zonal shift are available there (verify per Region).

Improvement plan: List every action in the failover and recovery runbooks and classify each API call as control plane or data plane. Replace control-plane steps with pre-provisioned resources and data-plane mechanisms such as Route 53 health checks or ARC zonal shift, pre-scale or reserve the capacity the remaining locations need, and prove the plan in a failover test that does not use the replaced control-plane actions.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>rp_no_cross_region_cp</code>: The critical path and recovery steps have no runtime dependency on another Region or on a global service's control plane</summary>

Good looks like: Requests and recovery steps make no runtime call that needs another Region or a global service's control plane, which the AWS Fault Isolation Boundaries whitepaper places in a single Region (for example IAM, Route 53 public DNS and CloudFront). Credentials come from Regional AWS STS endpoints, configuration and secrets are replicated or cached in the Region, and global-service changes are never a recovery step. Failing over to a recovery Region meets this, as long as the steps run in the recovery Region and need nothing from the impaired one. Platform notes: Some AWS SDKs and CLIs default to the global AWS STS endpoint, so set the STS Regional endpoints setting to regional. Avoid synchronous cross-Region calls on the critical path. Partition notes: In AWS GovCloud (US), as of 2026-10-06, the IAM and Route 53 control planes are in AWS GovCloud (US-West), so recovery steps in AWS GovCloud (US-East) must not change IAM or Route 53 configuration, and there is no global AWS STS endpoint.

Improvement plan: Trace each critical call and each recovery step to the Region and the plane it uses. Switch clients to Regional AWS STS endpoints, replicate or cache configuration and secrets in each Region, remove calls to global-service control planes from the request path and the runbooks, and create ahead of time anything those steps used to create.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>rp_no_shared_fate</code>: No configuration, credential or resource shared across AZs, cells or Regions can impair all of them at once</summary>

Good looks like: No single configuration value, credential, certificate, data store, deployment or network component is shared by every Availability Zone, cell or Region in a way that one bad change or one failure impairs all of them. Changes to anything shared roll out one fault boundary at a time, and each location keeps running on its last known good copy if the shared source is unavailable. Platform notes: Containers: one cluster spanning Availability Zones shares its control plane and add-ons, so larger workloads may use cells or more than one cluster. Serverless: one function configuration or alias serves every Availability Zone, so stage its changes. EC2: one launch template or AMI change can reach every Availability Zone at once unless the rollout is staged.

Improvement plan: List what more than one Availability Zone, cell or Region uses (for example a shared database, a global configuration file, one certificate, or one deployment that targets everything). Split those items per fault boundary where it matters, or stage their changes one boundary at a time with automatic rollback, and keep a last known good copy in each location.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>rp_prod_isolated</code>: Production runs in dedicated accounts isolated from non-production, with organization guardrails on logging and Regions</summary>

Good looks like: Production runs in AWS accounts used only for production, separate from development and test, and managed in AWS Organizations. Service control policies or equivalent guardrails stop anyone from turning off audit logging such as AWS CloudTrail and restrict use to approved Regions, and non-production identities and pipelines have no standing access to production.

Improvement plan: Move production resources into dedicated accounts under a production organizational unit, attach service control policies that deny disabling logging and deny unapproved Regions, and remove access paths that let non-production identities or pipelines change production.

</details>

Statement detail:

<details>
<summary><code>rt_timeouts</code>: Every remote call (AWS SDK, HTTP, database, third party, model) sets connect and request timeouts sized to observed latency</summary>

Good looks like: Every remote call, including AWS SDK clients, HTTP clients, database drivers, third-party APIs and model endpoints, sets an explicit connect timeout and request or socket timeout chosen from measured latency (for example just above the p99.9 latency) rather than from library defaults. The total time across all attempts fits inside the caller's own timeout, and timeouts are counted in a metric. Platform notes: EC2 and containers: also bound connection-pool wait times and worker or thread pools. Serverless: keep downstream timeouts shorter than the Lambda function timeout and any API Gateway integration timeout, and set task timeouts in AWS Step Functions state machines.

Improvement plan: Inventory every client and driver and record its current timeouts. Measure latency per dependency, set connect and request timeouts from those measurements instead of relying on defaults, emit a timeout metric per dependency, and add a test or code review check that fails when a client is created without explicit timeouts.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>rt_bounded_backoff</code>: Retries, including asynchronous ones, are capped and use exponential backoff with jitter</summary>

Good looks like: Every retry mechanism, including SDK retries, queue redelivery, Lambda asynchronous retries and Step Functions Retry fields, has a maximum number of attempts and waits with exponential backoff and jitter. Synchronous calls retry little, if at all, and return an error the client can act on; asynchronous work backs off for longer and then goes to a dead-letter queue that is alarmed. Platform notes: AWS SDKs: use the standard retry mode, which uses exponential backoff with jitter and a retry quota, and set maximum attempts deliberately. Serverless: set Lambda asynchronous retry attempts (0-2) and maximum event age, an Amazon SQS maxReceiveCount with a dead-letter queue, and Step Functions MaxAttempts, BackoffRate, MaxDelaySeconds and JitterStrategy.

Improvement plan: Find every retry mechanism in code, SDK configuration, queues, functions and workflows. Cap the attempts, add exponential backoff with jitter, send exhausted asynchronous work to a dead-letter queue with an alarm, and run a test with a dependency failing to confirm that retries do not multiply traffic.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>rt_retry_budget</code>: A retry budget or circuit breaker stops retries to unhealthy or throttling dependencies, and only one layer retries</summary>

Good looks like: Retries are limited as a share of traffic, with a retry budget or token bucket such as the AWS SDK retry quota, or stopped by a circuit breaker while a dependency is unhealthy or throttling, so retry traffic falls as errors rise. Only one layer in the call chain retries a given failure and the other layers fail fast. Calls to third-party APIs stay within their published rate limits.

Improvement plan: Map the call chain and remove retries from all but one layer. Enable a retry quota or budget in that layer. Add a circuit breaker only where a dependency can stay unhealthy for long periods, and test it, because circuit breakers add modal behavior. Alarm on the retry rate per dependency, and use client-side rate limiting to stay within third-party limits.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>rt_idempotent</code>: Retried mutating operations are idempotent (for example client tokens), and only failed batch items are retried</summary>

Good looks like: Every mutating operation that can be retried accepts an idempotency key or client token, so a retried create, payment or message has exactly one effect, and consumers of at-least-once queues and streams deduplicate. Batch consumers report only the failed items for retry instead of failing the whole batch. Platform notes: AWS APIs: Amazon EC2 and many other AWS APIs accept a client token for idempotent requests. Serverless: turn on partial batch responses (ReportBatchItemFailures) for Lambda event source mappings such as Amazon SQS.

Improvement plan: List the mutating APIs and message handlers. Add an idempotency key that the server stores with the result for a retention window, return the original result for duplicates, pass client tokens on AWS API calls that accept them, and switch batch handlers to report partial failures.

</details>

<summary><code>tp_per_source_limits</code>: Per-client, tenant, API key or source limits are enforced (or there is one trusted, bounded caller)</summary>

</details>

Platform notes: EC2 and containers: concurrency limits in the server and connection limits at the load balancer. </details>

Good looks like: An operator can raise or lower a limit, or block one abusive caller, within minutes through validated, versioned configuration that is rolled out safely, without building or deploying code. The steps are in the runbook and work during an incident. Platform notes: Typical levers: block a caller with AWS WAF IP sets or rate-based rules; change limits through dynamic configuration such as AWS AppConfig; tune per-client fairness with API Gateway usage plans (best-effort, not hard limits).

<details>
<summary><code>dc_pitr</code>: Point-in-time recovery, versioning or frequent backups protect every critical data store</summary>

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>dc_immutable_isolated</code>: Backups are immutable and isolated from production credentials, ideally in a separate account (for example Vault Lock)</summary>

Platform notes: AWS Backup logically air-gapped vaults combine locked retention with sharing for recovery into another account. Partition notes: AWS Backup logically air-gapped vaults are not available in AWS GovCloud (US) as of 2026-10-05; use compliance-mode Vault Lock on a vault in a separate backup account with cross-account copy, or S3 Object Lock in compliance mode; verify per Region.

Improvement plan: Create a dedicated backup account, copy critical recovery points to a locked vault there (compliance-mode Vault Lock or a logically air-gapped vault), restrict who can change backup and key policies with permission guardrails such as service control policies, keep backup keys out of reach of production administrators, and alarm on attempts to delete recovery points or change vault, lock or key settings. Test that you can restore from the isolated copy into a clean account.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>dc_detection</code>: Integrity checks or alarms detect anomalous deletes, writes or encryption of critical data</summary>

Good looks like: The team learns about corruption, mass deletion or ransomware-style encryption in time to restore from a point before it began: alarms watch unusual delete or overwrite rates, write volume, record or object counts and permission or encryption changes; scheduled integrity checks (checksums, invariants, reconciliation with a source of record) run on critical data; threat detection flags anomalous data access; and recovery points are scanned for malware before use. Alarms page the data owner, and the runbook says how to find the last known-good point. Platform notes: Examples: Amazon GuardDuty S3 Protection findings for anomalous deletes; GuardDuty Malware Protection for AWS Backup to scan recovery points; CloudWatch alarms on integrity metrics; Amazon S3 checksums. Partition notes: In AWS GovCloud (US), GuardDuty Malware Protection for AWS Backup does not scan Amazon EC2 or Amazon EBS recovery points as of 2026-10-06; scan restored copies in an isolated account instead; verify per Region.

Improvement plan: For each critical store, define what abnormal looks like (delete and overwrite rates, record counts, write volume, encryption or permission changes) and alarm on it; add scheduled integrity checks that compare data with its source of record; turn on threat detection for data stores; scan recovery points for malware; and document in the runbook how to identify the last known-good recovery point.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>dc_point_restore_rehearsed</code>: Restoring to a point before a simulated corruption was rehearsed, and the restored data was validated</summary>

Good looks like: The team rehearsed a corruption scenario in a non-production copy, for example a bad batch job, a faulty migration or mass-deleted records: it identified the last known-good point, restored to it from the isolated backup copy, validated the restored data with integrity checks and a critical journey, and reconciled or replayed changes made after that point. Time to detect, decide and restore was recorded against RTO and RPO, and the runbook was updated. Partition notes: AWS Backup restore testing is not available in AWS GovCloud (US) as of 2026-10-05; script the restore and validation steps instead; verify per Region.

Improvement plan: Add a corruption drill to the recovery test plan: in a non-production copy, inject a realistic corruption or deletion, practice finding the last known-good point, restore from the isolated backup copy into a clean environment, run validation, and reconcile later changes. Record the timings, fix gaps in tooling and runbooks, and repeat at least yearly.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>pd_staged_expansion</code>: Each production change, including configuration and flags, first reaches a small slice, then expands in automated, gated stages</summary>

<details>
<summary><code>pd_fault_boundary_waves</code>: Multi-Region or cell-based workloads roll out one Region or cell at a time, and single-Region workloads use percentage stages</summary>

Good looks like: Multi-Region or cell-based workloads deploy to one Region or cell first and then to the rest in waves of increasing size, so a bad change is much more likely to be caught in one Region or cell before it reaches the rest. The order of Regions, cells or zones is fixed in the pipeline definition, not left to the operator. Platform notes: EC2: AWS CodeDeploy zonal configuration deploys to one Availability Zone at a time, with a monitor duration between zones. </details>

<details>
<summary><code>pd_bake_time</code>: Each stage bakes under monitoring for longer than its rollback alarms take to evaluate, longest in early stages</summary>

Good looks like: After each stage completes, the rollout waits for a defined bake time while automated monitoring watches alarms and canaries, and it promotes only if they stay clear. Each stage's bake time exceeds the evaluation time of the rollback alarms it watches (the alarm period multiplied by its evaluation periods, plus any delay in the metric arriving), so an alarm can fire before the next stage starts. The first stages, where a change first meets real traffic, bake longest, long enough to surface slow-burning problems and to include representative traffic; later stages can bake for less, but never for less than that evaluation time. Platform notes: EC2: the CodeDeploy zonal monitor duration, with a longer first-zone duration. Containers: Amazon ECS bake time in blue/green, linear and canary deployments. Serverless: the interval in CodeDeploy canary or linear configurations for Lambda aliases. Configuration and flags: the AWS AppConfig bake time after a deployment reaches all targets.

Improvement plan: For every production stage, list the rollback alarms it watches and their evaluation time, then set a bake time longer than the slowest of them, longest in the first stages. </details>

<details>
<summary><code>pd_blockers</code>: Automated blockers stop deployments outside approved windows, on blocked or peak dates, and during active alarms</summary>

Good looks like: The pipeline itself refuses to start a production stage outside approved deployment windows (for example when the on-call team is not staffed to respond), on dates the business has blocked, such as peak or event days, and while relevant alarms are firing. A documented, audited override exists for urgent fixes. Platform notes: AWS CodePipeline: stage entry conditions with the DeploymentWindow rule (a cron expression) and the CloudWatchAlarm rule. Other CI/CD systems: an equivalent check before each production stage against a change calendar and the current alarm state.

Improvement plan: Add a check before each production stage that blocks deployments outside the approved window, on blocked dates and while customer-facing alarms are in ALARM, and document how an urgent fix overrides it with an audit record. Give the blocked-date calendar an owner and keep it current.

</details>

3. `HIGH_RISK`: `default`

Statement detail:

<details>
<summary><code>slo_defined</code>: Availability, latency and async-completion SLOs are defined for each critical user journey and API</summary>

Good looks like: Each critical user journey and API has written SLOs measured from the customer's side: availability (successful requests over valid requests), latency at a high percentile (for example p99), and, for asynchronous work, time to completion. Each SLO has an owner, a target, a measurement window and an error budget, is derived from business needs, and is tighter than any SLA. CloudWatch Application Signals can track latency and availability SLOs, or you can compute them from your own metrics. Partition notes: As of 2026-10-06, CloudWatch Application Signals is available in AWS GovCloud (US) (AWS Capabilities by Region) but not for Lambda functions (AWS GovCloud (US) User Guide); compute Lambda SLOs from your own CloudWatch metrics. Verify per Region. Reader note: This page mentions AWS Service Catalog AppRegistry, which is closed to new customers; associating an SLO with an application there is optional and is not needed for this practice.

Improvement plan: For each critical journey and API, agree availability, latency-percentile and completion-time targets with the business owner, set them tighter than any SLA, choose the metric that measures each one from the customer's side, and publish them with an owner and an error budget.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>slo_alarms_before_breach</code>: Paging alarms on customer-facing error rates and latency percentiles fire before the SLO or SLA is breached</summary>

Good looks like: Every SLO has at least one alarm that pages the on-call engineer when the error rate or latency percentile crosses a threshold, or the error budget burns at a rate, that leaves time to act before the SLO or SLA is breached. Alarms use rates and percentiles (for example server errors over requests, p99 latency) rather than averages or raw counts, and cover each Region and entry point. Informational alarms go to a ticket queue, not a pager. Platform notes: EC2 and containers behind a load balancer: Application Load Balancer metrics such as HTTPCode_Target_5XX_Count and TargetResponseTime, with metric math for rates. Serverless: API Gateway 5XXError and Latency, and Lambda Errors and Duration. Asynchronous journeys: alarm on completion time or success rate, not only on API errors. Reader note: This page refers to Amazon DevOps Guru (closed to new customers from 2026-10-29, end of support 2027-09-30); CloudWatch alarms, composite alarms and CloudWatch anomaly detection meet this practice.

Improvement plan: Create an alarm for each SLO on error rate and on latency percentile, with thresholds that fire while error budget remains; consider a fast-burn and a slow-burn alarm per SLO. Route them to the paging rotation, test each one by setting it to ALARM, and keep alarm definitions in IaC.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>slo_alarm_quality</code>: Critical alarms use short periods (for example 1 minute), deliberate missing-data handling and M-of-N evaluation</summary>

Good looks like: Critical alarms evaluate short periods (for example 1 minute) so they fire within minutes, use M out of N datapoints to alarm (for example 3 of 5) so a single spike does not page, and set missing-data treatment on purpose: breaching for signals that must always report, such as request-count or success metrics that should never drop to zero (canary and heartbeat alarms are scored in event_canary_alarms), and not breaching or ignore where gaps are normal. Composite alarms or anomaly detection reduce noise where static thresholds do not fit.

Improvement plan: Review every paging alarm's period, datapoints to alarm and missing-data treatment, record the reason for each choice, fix alarms that page on a single datapoint or stay quiet when data stops, and check these settings in code review of the alarm IaC.

</details>

<details>
<summary><code>slo_reviewed</code>: SLO attainment, error budgets and noisy alarms are reviewed in a recurring operations review and drive fixes</summary>

Good looks like: A weekly or other regular operations review looks at SLO attainment and error budget burn, alarms that fired without needing action or failed to fire, recent pages and open action items, and turns findings into tracked fixes to thresholds, runbooks or the workload. Attendance includes the people who are on call. A sample agenda is in docs/ops-review-agenda.md in this lens's repository.

Improvement plan: Schedule a recurring operations review with a fixed agenda: SLO attainment and error budget, alarms that fired and whether they were actionable, missed detections, and open action items. Give each fix an owner and a due date and track it to closure.

</details>

<summary><code>tr_correlated_logs</code>: Structured logs carry request or trace IDs, appear in error responses, and are centrally queryable during incidents</summary>

Reader note: This page refers to Amazon DevOps Guru (closed to new customers from 2026-10-29, end of support 2027-09-30); alarm actions, Amazon EventBridge rules and Systems Manager Automation runbooks meet this practice.
